What Do They Really Think?

Most of us carry around a picture of “the other side.” Guess what Americans in each party believe and who they are, then see how close you got. You’ll guess about both parties, so it doesn’t matter which side you’re on, or if you’re on neither.

Why it matters

Both studies found that Americans picture the other party as more extreme and more unusual than it really is. More in Common found that Democrats and Republicans imagined almost twice as many people on the other side held extreme views as really do. People with bigger perception gaps also felt more negatively toward the other side.

There’s good news in the same research. Most people on each side agree with plenty of statements the other side assumes they reject. When Ahler and Sood told people what the other party actually looks like, they saw its members as less extreme and felt less socially distant from them.

Talk about it

  1. Where does your picture of each party come from? Think about who you know, what you watch, and what you scroll past.
  2. More in Common found that Democrats with more education had larger perception gaps, and that the most educated Democrats were the most likely to say most of their friends share their politics. What does that suggest about social networks and stereotypes?
  3. The groups people overestimated are the ones they associate with each party. How is this like stereotypes about other groups we study, such as racial or gender groups?
  4. These surveys were done in 2015 and 2018. Would the gaps be bigger or smaller today? How could you find out?

About the data

Round 1: More in Common surveyed 2,100 U.S. adults online with YouGov on November 7–10, 2018, right after the midterm elections (936 Democrats, 755 Republicans, 409 independents; margin of error ±2.1 points overall). Each partisan’s guess shown here is the average estimate from the other party.

Round 2: Ahler and Sood’s guesses come from a YouGov survey of 1,000 Americans in March 2015. The actual shares come mostly from the 2012 American National Election Study, with Pew Research Center data for religion. Both parties’ makeup has changed since then.

Sources: Yudkin, Daniel, Stephen Hawkins, and Tim Dixon. 2019. The Perception Gap: How False Impressions Are Pulling Americans Apart. More in Common. Ahler, Douglas J., and Gaurav Sood. 2018. “The Parties in Our Heads: Misperceptions about Party Composition and Their Consequences.” Journal of Politics 80(3): 964–981.
